# 3  Adjust your week

## 3.1 Start with a simple log

After each run, record how many minutes you ran, how many of those were controlled repetitions, and how the run felt. Note anything that affected it, such as heat or poor sleep. The [counting convention](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#count-the-time) applies here too. A notebook is enough.

| Entry | Example |
|:---|:---|
| Session | Wednesday, introductory workout |
| Running / quality | 30 minutes total, including 12 minutes of repetitions |
| Effort | Controlled early; harder in the last two repetitions |
| Conditions | Warmer than usual |
| Recovery | Poor sleep; legs still heavy the next morning |
| Next decision | Keep Thursday as rest; check recovery before Saturday’s easy run |

This log follows the [three-day example](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#first-workout-week). Add the recovery note the next day; you cannot know it at the end of the workout.

Look for patterns across sessions. A single pace, heart-rate reading, or dashboard colour cannot tell you whether the week is working. Optional [load metrics](https://norwegiansingles.run/section3_training_load.html#training-load) can add context once the basic log is useful.

## 3.2 Adjust your week

### 3.2.1 You missed a session

Keep the next run as planned, provided you have recovered. In the three-day example, missing Wednesday’s introductory workout means Saturday stays easy. There is no need to catch up on the repetitions.

In a week with more workouts, the same rule applies: if Tuesday’s workout is missed, keep Wednesday easy and do Thursday’s planned workout only if you feel ready. Moving Tuesday’s work to Wednesday would put two workouts on consecutive days.

After several missed days, begin with easy running that feels comfortable now. Reintroduce a workout when those runs feel normal, using a smaller week if needed.

### 3.2.2 Your legs feel unusually heavy

Review what changed: faster repetitions, more running, a race, heat, strength work, or less sleep. Make the next quality day easy or rest, then reassess. If normal easy running still feels unusually hard, keep reducing the load until easy running feels normal again.

For example, in the [five-hour week](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#standard-week), replacing Thursday’s 50-minute quality session with 30 minutes easy produces 280 minutes running and 48 minutes quality. Saturday is conditional on recovery; cancel or shorten it if the fatigue persists. This is a worked adjustment, not a requirement to complete the remaining quality minutes.

### 3.2.3 You have pain or are ill

Stop a session if pain changes how you run or worsens as you continue. Rest when illness makes training inappropriate, and seek medical advice for persistent or concerning symptoms. Do not use a lower calculated pace as permission to train through them. When returning, begin with comfortable easy running and rebuild from what you can do now. Missed work does not need to be recovered.

### 3.2.4 You have less time this week

For example, if Friday becomes unavailable in the [four-day week](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#transition-week), skip Friday’s B workout. Keep Monday’s A, Wednesday easy, and Sunday easy if recovered. Your week becomes 140 running minutes, including 18 quality minutes. Saturday remains a rest day; you do not need to move the missed workout there.

Remove a quality session before trying to fit several demanding days together. A shorter version of a session is another option when there is enough time to warm up and cool down. Reduce the week as a whole if necessary; running fewer minutes is preferable to compressing the entire schedule into consecutive hard days.

### 3.2.5 A good dashboard disagrees with how you feel

Act on the poor recovery. Check recent pace and volume changes, then replace the next workout with easy running or rest. A training-load score estimates the amount of training you have recorded; it does not measure whether your legs, sleep, and daily life are coping with it.

## 3.3 When progress stalls

Before adding training, compare like with like. A hot race on a hilly route is a poor test of progress against a cool, flat one. Review whether the recent result reflects comparable conditions and preparation.

Then check the routine. Are easy days easy enough? Have repetitions become faster than the plan? Did you add quality work, long-run time, and strength work together? Have sleep or meals become less consistent? Correct the problem you find and repeat a manageable week before making another change.

If your workouts and recovery look good, keep the same amount of training for now. Use a later race or time trial to assess progress, with recovery planned around it. A prolonged unexplained decline or persistent fatigue warrants further assessment.

## 3.4 Food, sleep, and life outside running

Make room for regular meals and enough food to support the running you do. Carbohydrate-containing meals and snacks around demanding days can help you arrive fueled and replenish afterward. Practise eating and drinking during longer runs before relying on a strategy in a race.

Allow enough time for sleep regularly and account for work, caring responsibilities, and other demands when choosing a week. If these demands increase, holding or reducing training is a practical adjustment. Weight loss and calorie restriction are not part of this guide’s training progression. The [IOC consensus on low energy availability](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/17/1073) provides background on the risks when intake does not meet exercise demands.

## 3.5 What to expect

The appeal of this approach is a familiar routine with controlled workouts and modest equipment needs. Repetition can also become monotonous, and keeping effort under control takes practice. There is no promised date for a personal best, and the schedule cannot guarantee freedom from injury.

Use [Progression](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#progression) when your current week meets its recovery checks, or [Race adaptations](https://norwegiansingles.run/section5_applicability_distances.html#race-adaptations) when preparing for an event.

You now have the instructions needed to plan a week, run a workout, and decide what to do afterward. The chapters on tools, training scores, and comparisons are optional; return to them if you have a question they can help answer.
