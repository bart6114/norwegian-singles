# Adjust your week {#adjust-training}

## Start with a simple log {#monitoring}

After each run, record total running time, quality minutes, perceived effort, and anything that affected the session. The [counting convention](section2_implementing_the_method.md#count-the-time) applies here too. A notebook is enough.

| Entry | Example |
|:------|:--------|
| Session | Tuesday, card A |
| Running / quality | 50 / 24 minutes |
| Effort | Controlled early; harder in the last two repetitions |
| Conditions | Warmer than usual |
| Recovery | Poor sleep; legs still heavy the next morning |
| Next decision | Keep Wednesday short and easy; reassess before Thursday |

Look for patterns across sessions. A single pace, heart-rate reading, or dashboard colour cannot tell you whether the week is working. Optional [load metrics](section3_training_load.md#training-load) can add context once the basic log is useful.

## Adjust your week {#adjust-your-week}

### You missed a session

Let it go. If Tuesday's quality session was missed, keep Wednesday easy and do Thursday's planned session only if you feel ready. Do not move Tuesday's work to Wednesday and then keep Thursday unchanged. After several missed days, restart with a smaller week that fits your current condition.

### Your legs feel unusually heavy

Review what changed: faster repetitions, more running, a race, heat, strength work, or less sleep. Make the next quality day easy or rest, then reassess. If normal easy running still feels unusually hard, keep reducing the load instead of trying to recover a dashboard score.

For example, in the [five-hour week](section2_implementing_the_method.md#standard-week), replacing Thursday's 50-minute quality session with 30 minutes easy produces 280 minutes running and 48 minutes quality. Saturday is conditional on recovery; cancel or shorten it if the fatigue persists. This is a worked adjustment, not a requirement to complete the remaining quality minutes.

### You have pain or are ill

Stop a session if pain changes how you run or worsens as you continue. Rest when illness makes training inappropriate, and seek medical advice for persistent or concerning symptoms. Do not use a lower calculated pace as permission to train through them. When returning, rebuild from what you can comfortably do now rather than trying to recover missed work.

### You have less time this week

Remove a quality session before trying to fit several demanding days together. A shorter version of a session is another option when there is enough time to warm up and cool down. Reduce the week as a whole if necessary; running fewer minutes is preferable to compressing the entire schedule into consecutive hard days.

### A good dashboard disagrees with how you feel

Act on the poor recovery. Check recent pace and volume changes, then replace the next workout with easy running or rest. CTL and similar metrics describe recorded training load; they do not measure whether your legs, sleep, and daily life are coping with it.

## When progress stalls {#stagnation}

Before adding training, compare like with like. A hot race on a hilly route is a poor test of progress against a cool, flat one. Review whether the recent result reflects comparable conditions and preparation.

Then check the routine. Are easy days easy enough? Have repetitions become faster than the plan? Did you add quality work, long-run time, and strength work together? Have sleep or meals become less consistent? Correct the problem you find and repeat a manageable week before making another change.

If execution and recovery look good, maintaining the current dose is a valid decision. Use a later race or time trial to assess progress, with recovery planned around it. A prolonged unexplained decline or persistent fatigue warrants further assessment. A guessed muscle-fiber type is not a useful basis for prescribing more training.

## Food, sleep, and life outside running {#recovery}

Make room for regular meals and enough food to support the running you do. Carbohydrate-containing meals and snacks around demanding days can help you arrive fueled and replenish afterward. Practise eating and drinking during longer runs before relying on a strategy in a race.

Keep a regular sleep opportunity and account for work, caring responsibilities, and other demands when choosing a week. If these demands increase, holding or reducing training is a practical adjustment. Weight loss and calorie restriction are not part of this guide's training progression. The [IOC consensus on low energy availability](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/57/17/1073) provides background on the risks when intake does not meet exercise demands.

## What to expect {#pros}

The appeal of this approach is a familiar routine with controlled workouts and modest equipment needs. Repetition can also become monotonous, and keeping effort under control takes practice. There is no promised date for a personal best, and the schedule cannot guarantee freedom from injury.

Use [Progression](section2_implementing_the_method.md#progression) when the current week has become repeatable, or [Race adaptations](section5_applicability_distances.md#race-adaptations) when preparing for an event.
