# Control your effort {#control-effort}

## Sub-threshold focus {#sub-threshold-focus}

The standard week alternates three quality sessions with easy running and finishes with a longer easy run. During quality work, the aim is a controlled effort below the second lactate threshold (LT2). Breaking the work into repetitions makes it possible to accumulate useful running time while keeping the session manageable.

The test of the routine is whether you can recover and repeat it. Finishing one workout faster than planned does not help if it leaves you unable to run the next one as intended. Start with [a week that fits your current training](section2_implementing_the_method.md#choose-your-week).

## Intensity control {#intensity-control}

Use this sequence for each quality session:

1. Choose a starting pace from a recent race or time trial using the [LacTrace Norwegian Singles guide](https://lactrace.com/norwegian-singles). Select the range for the repetition length you will run. Use current fitness, including any loss of fitness after a break.
2. Warm up easily, then begin the first repetition under control. Let the effort settle before deciding whether the pace fits the day.
3. Read pace alongside breathing, perceived effort, and heart rate if you measure it. Check the pattern across repetitions. A pace that felt comfortable early may become too demanding later.
4. Slow down if the session is getting much harder than intended. In heat, wind, or on hills, accept a slower pace. If slowing does not restore control, shorten the workout or finish with easy running. Stop for pain or illness symptoms that make continuing inappropriate.
5. Finish without a sprint or a maximal final repetition. Record how it felt and whether you recovered before the next quality day.

In the original discussion, sirpoc describes using pace estimates and checking the response over the workout, with heart rate rising during the early repetitions. His numbers describe his own training, not everyone's limits. [Original account, post #450](https://www.letsrun.com/forum/flat_read.php?thread=12130781&page=22).

### Why not chase heart rate in the first repetition?

Heart rate takes time to respond. Speeding up to reach a number immediately can make the opening repetitions too hard. Begin at the planned controlled pace and assess the trend as the workout develops. An unusually low reading can also be a sensor problem; it is not a reason to accelerate blindly.

If you have a credible individual threshold-heart-rate estimate, use it as a check on the later repetitions. An age-based maximum-heart-rate formula or a watch's default zone is not a precise personal threshold. You can run these sessions using pace and effort while you learn your response.

### Why are short repetitions faster than long ones?

The pace estimate belongs to a particular work duration and recovery. A three-minute repetition and a twelve-minute repetition need not use the same pace. Another calculator's continuous threshold pace is answering a different question. Compare the whole session before comparing the numbers.

Use the linked guide consistently, and slow down if its estimate feels too demanding. A recovery break does not guarantee that a fast repetition stays below threshold. The [session cards](section2_implementing_the_method.md#session-cards) keep work duration, recovery, and weekly dose together.

### One effort scale

This guide uses perceived exertion on a 1-10 scale, where 1 is very light and 10 is maximal. Easy runs should allow comfortable conversation. Quality work should feel controlled and purposeful, roughly 5-6, with enough breath for a short phrase and enough reserve for another repetition. These are descriptive checks, not measurements of LT2. If your breathing becomes strained or you need to race the finish of each repetition, slow down.

Lactate is optional. There is no universal blood-lactate value that guarantees you are below LT2: [Bakken describes substantial individual differences](https://www.mariusbakken.com/the-norwegian-model.html). Testing needs an individual reference and a consistent protocol. See [Tools & optional additions](section4_individualization_considerations.md#tools-and-additions).

## Easy recovery {#easy-recovery}

Easy days should leave you ready for the next quality session. Keep the longer run at the same easy effort; it is not a weekly fast finish.

The practice described by sirpoc uses roughly 70% of maximum heart rate as an upper guide for easy running. That percentage depends on a credible maximum and a reliable measurement. His [early explanation, post #170](https://www.letsrun.com/forum/flat_read.php?thread=12130781&page=8), describes how he used it in his own training. There is no single easy pace that follows from a 5 km result for every runner.

| What you notice | What to do on this run |
|:---------------|:-----------------------|
| Heart rate rises on a hill | Slow down or walk the steep part. Choose flatter routes when possible. |
| Heat makes the usual pace harder | Reduce pace and, if needed, duration. Compare effort with similar conditions. |
| The reading suddenly jumps | Check sensor fit and compare with breathing and effort before changing pace. |
| Easy running feels awkwardly slow | Check the maximum-heart-rate estimate and sensor first. Try flatter ground or short walk breaks; shorten the run if the movement remains uncomfortable. |
| You feel unrecovered from quality work | Shorten the run or rest. Reconsider the next quality session using [Adjust your week](section6_benefits_monitoring.md#adjust-your-week). |

Without heart-rate data, use comfortable conversation and relaxed breathing, then check how you feel later that day and before the next workout. An individual adjustment should still serve the purpose of the easy day.

## Consistency & repeatability {#consistency-repeatability}

Keep the week stable long enough to learn how you respond. More work is only useful when you can absorb it. [Progression](section2_implementing_the_method.md#progression) explains how to change the dose without changing everything at once.
