# 2  Plan your week & run the workouts

## 2.1 Keep your own running days

Start with a recent ordinary week you could comfortably repeat. Use your actual running time, rather than the hours you hope to train eventually. If you are new to intervals, first read [how the effort should feel](https://norwegiansingles.run/section1_core_principles.html#intensity-control).

1.  Write down your running days, the minutes you usually run on each, and your rest days. For now, keep those days and time limits.
2.  Choose an existing run to replace with a controlled workout. If you are new to intervals, start with one. If you already handle controlled workouts comfortably, the two-workout example may fit. The full routine has three, but you do not need to begin there.
3.  Leave at least one easy or rest day between workouts, including across the end of the week. If you already do a hard group run, hills, or a race, replace or remove that demanding session when introducing these workouts.
4.  Choose a complete workout that fits the time available on that day. Its warm-up, recovery jogs, and cool-down are included. Keep the other runs easy, and keep your longest run at a duration you already handle.

For example, if your usual run is 30 minutes, use the [30-minute introductory workout](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#first-session). Do not extend it to 40 or 50 minutes just because another table uses that length. If even the introductory workout is beyond your current running, continue with easy runs while establishing a comfortable routine.

The examples below show how this planning works. You can move their weekdays to match your life, while keeping easy or rest days between workouts. There is no requirement to progress through every table or to reach seven running days.

### 2.1.1 Choose the example closest to your current routine

| Your current running | Where to look |
|:---|:---|
| Regular easy runs; little or no interval experience | [Three days, introducing one workout](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#first-workout-week) |
| Already comfortable with controlled workouts; about three hours over four days | [Four days with two workouts](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#transition-week) |
| Already used to three workouts; about four hours over six days | [Six days with a rest day](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#rest-day-week) |
| About five hours over seven days, with experience recovering between workouts | [Full three-workout routine](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#standard-week) |
| Already sustaining seven hours and three controlled workouts | [Larger week](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#larger-week) |

These are examples written for this guide, not individual prescriptions or schedules copied from a book. Their repetition structure draws on sirpoc’s [discussion of pacing and scaling work to weekly running](https://www.letsrun.com/forum/flat_read.php?thread=12130781&page=28). The introductory workout and smaller weeks show ways to learn the approach before considering the full routine.

### 2.1.2 How to read the tables

All times are minutes. **Running includes Quality**: a 40-minute workout with 18 minutes of quality work takes 40 minutes in total, not 58. The remaining minutes are easy running. Rest means no running.

[Intro](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#first-session), [A](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-a), [B](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-b), and [C](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-c) link to the workout instructions later in this chapter. A, B, and C each have a shorter (40-minute), standard (50-minute), and larger (60-minute) version. Choose only the version named in your week. The percentages in the total rows describe the examples; you do not need to reach them.

### 2.1.3 Three days: introduce one workout

Suppose you already run easily for 30 minutes on Monday, Wednesday, and Saturday, and recover comfortably. Keep all three days and their duration. Change Wednesday to the [introductory workout](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#first-session); Monday and Saturday stay easy. Your four rest days stay free. None of your runs needs to get longer to try this.

| Day       | Session                  | Running | Quality |
|:----------|:-------------------------|--------:|--------:|
| Monday    | Easy                     |      30 |       0 |
| Tuesday   | Rest                     |       0 |       0 |
| Wednesday | Intro: first workout     |      30 |      12 |
| Thursday  | Rest                     |       0 |       0 |
| Friday    | Rest                     |       0 |       0 |
| Saturday  | Easy                     |      30 |       0 |
| Sunday    | Rest                     |       0 |       0 |
| Total     | 1.5 hours; 13.3% quality |      90 |      12 |

Before the change, all 90 minutes were easy. Afterward, 12 of those same 90 minutes are controlled repetitions. You have changed the effort of part of one run, without adding running time or another day.

After Wednesday, note whether the repetitions stayed controlled. On Saturday, check whether your usual easy run feels normal. If you still feel unusually tired, shorten it or rest, and make the next planned workout easy. Repeat this one-workout arrangement while learning how you respond; one successful session is not a reason to add another immediately.

### 2.1.4 Four days: two controlled workouts

This example is for someone already comfortable running 40 minutes on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, plus 60 minutes on Sunday. They have experience with controlled intervals and recover well enough to consider two workouts. It is a different starting routine from the three-day example, not the next week’s target.

Replace Monday’s run with [shorter A](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-a) and Friday’s with [shorter B](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-b). Keep Wednesday and Sunday easy, with the same rest days and the same three-hour total. If you are new to intervals, make Friday an easy 40 minutes and try just Monday’s workout first; that gives 18 quality minutes for the week instead of 36.

| Day       | Session              | Running | Quality |
|:----------|:---------------------|--------:|--------:|
| Monday    | A: shorter           |      40 |      18 |
| Tuesday   | Rest                 |       0 |       0 |
| Wednesday | Easy                 |      40 |       0 |
| Thursday  | Rest                 |       0 |       0 |
| Friday    | B: shorter           |      40 |      18 |
| Saturday  | Rest                 |       0 |       0 |
| Sunday    | Longer easy          |      60 |       0 |
| Total     | 3 hours; 20% quality |     180 |      36 |

Use A and B again when repeating this week; there is no need to rotate in C or fit in a third workout. If Sunday’s run leaves you tired on Monday, shorten Sunday or make Monday easy. Separation on a calendar does not guarantee that you have recovered.

### 2.1.5 Six days: keep a rest day

This four-hour example is for someone already used to six running days and three controlled workouts. Friday stays free. It is not an introductory week for someone who has only done easy runs. If you need fewer workouts, make Saturday easy instead, or use the smaller examples above.

| Day       | Session                | Running | Quality |
|:----------|:-----------------------|--------:|--------:|

| Tuesday   | A: shorter             |      40 |      18 |
| Wednesday | Easy                   |      30 |       0 |

| Saturday  | C: shorter             |      40 |      18 |
| Sunday    | Longer easy            |      60 |       0 |
| Total     | 4 hours; 22.5% quality |     240 |      54 |

### 2.1.6 Full routine: five hours over seven days

This illustrates the full three-workout routine for someone already comfortable with about five hours over seven days and experienced in recovering between controlled workouts. Use the standard 50-minute versions of [A](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-a), [B](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-b), and [C](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#session-c).

| Day       | Session               | Running | Quality |
|:----------|:----------------------|--------:|--------:|
| Monday    | Easy                  |      30 |       0 |
| Tuesday   | A: short repetitions  |      50 |      24 |
| Wednesday | Easy                  |      30 |       0 |
| Thursday  | B: medium repetitions |      50 |      24 |
| Friday    | Easy                  |      30 |       0 |
| Saturday  | C: long repetitions   |      50 |      24 |
| Sunday    | Longer easy           |      60 |       0 |
| Total     | 5 hours; 24% quality  |     300 |      72 |

If you already run this much but are learning the workouts, keep Saturday easy for a two-workout week, or keep Thursday and Saturday easy for a one-workout week. Running time remains 300 minutes; quality time becomes 48 or 24 minutes respectively. You can also use the shorter workout versions and accept a smaller weekly total.

## 2.2 Run your chosen workout

A “session card” is simply a complete set of workout instructions. Use the card and version you chose for your own week. You do one version of one card on a workout day.

Run the repetitions at a controlled effort: breathing more deeply than on an easy run, still able to say a short phrase, and finishing with enough left for another repetition. A recent race can help estimate pace, but you can start by effort alone. Use [the pace and effort instructions](https://norwegiansingles.run/section1_core_principles.html#intensity-control) for either route.

Jog very easily during the breaks; their purpose is to recover before the next repetition. Warm-up and cool-down times are included in each total. If you need more time to warm up, leave out some work or choose an easier run so the session still fits your day. There is no need to complete every repetition if the effort becomes strained.

### 2.2.1 Intro: a 30-minute first workout

For a regular runner who comfortably handles 30-minute easy runs and wants to learn controlled repetitions. This is the workout used in the three-day example.

1.  Run easily for 10 minutes.
2.  Run for 3 minutes at a restrained, controlled effort, then jog very easily for 1 minute.
3.  Repeat that three-minute run and one-minute jog twice more. You have now done three repetitions and three recovery jogs.
4.  Run a fourth three-minute repetition, then go straight into 5 minutes of easy running to cool down.

Time check: 10 + (4 x 3) + (3 x 1) + 5 = 30 minutes running; 12 minutes quality.

That is what “4 x 3 minutes with 1-minute recoveries” means: four work periods with three breaks between them. If you lose control of breathing or effort, slow down. If it still feels too hard, stop the repetitions and finish easily if comfortable. The aim of the first attempt is to learn the effort, not to prove you can finish all four.

### 2.2.2 A: short repetitions

A is the first workout in the example weeks. Its short repetitions give you frequent breaks to check your effort. Use it on whichever day you planned; the weekday does not change the workout.

The numbered instructions are the 50-minute version. If your week says “shorter” or “larger,” use that version below instead.

1.  Run easily for 10 minutes.
2.  Run 8 repetitions of 3 minutes at a controlled effort, using your starting pace estimate if you have one. Jog easily for 1 minute between repetitions.
3.  Run easily for 9 minutes to cool down.

Time check: 10 + (8 x 3) + (7 x 1) + 9 = 50 minutes running; 24 minutes quality.

**Shorter version: 40 minutes total.** Warm up for 10 minutes, run 6 x 3 minutes with five 1-minute jogs, and cool down for 7 minutes.

Time check: 10 + (6 x 3) + (5 x 1) + 7 = 40 minutes running; 18 minutes quality.

**Larger version: 60 minutes total.** Warm up for 12 minutes, run 10 x 3 minutes with nine 1-minute jogs, and cool down for 9 minutes.

Time check: 12 + (10 x 3) + (9 x 1) + 9 = 60 minutes running; 30 minutes quality.

If you are straining to finish the early repetitions or need longer breaks to hold the pace, slow down. Stop the work early if control does not return; keep the cool-down easy if you are well enough to continue.

### 2.2.3 B: medium repetitions

B is the second workout in the example weeks. Each repetition lasts six minutes, so you may need a slower pace than for A to keep the same controlled effort.

The numbered instructions are the 50-minute version. Use the shorter or larger version only when that is what you planned.

1.  Run easily for 12 minutes.
2.  Run 4 repetitions of 6 minutes at a controlled effort, with 1 minute of easy jogging between them.
3.  Run easily for 11 minutes to cool down.

Time check: 12 + (4 x 6) + (3 x 1) + 11 = 50 minutes running; 24 minutes quality.

**Shorter version: 40 minutes total.** Warm up for 10 minutes, run 3 x 6 minutes with two 1-minute jogs, and cool down for 10 minutes.

Time check: 10 + (3 x 6) + (2 x 1) + 10 = 40 minutes running; 18 minutes quality.

**Larger version: 60 minutes total.** Warm up for 12 minutes, run 5 x 6 minutes with four 1-minute jogs, and cool down for 14 minutes.

Time check: 12 + (5 x 6) + (4 x 1) + 14 = 60 minutes running; 30 minutes quality.

If each repetition becomes noticeably harder at the same pace, slow down before the next one. You should finish feeling able to do another repetition.

### 2.2.4 C: long repetitions

C is the third workout in the three-workout examples. “Long” refers to each repetition; this is different from your longer easy run. You may need a slower pace than for A or B to keep the effort controlled.

The numbered instructions are the 50-minute version. If using a pace estimate, choose it for the repetition length in your version: nine, ten, or twelve minutes.

1.  Run easily for 12 minutes.
2.  Run 2 repetitions of 12 minutes at a controlled effort, with 1 minute of easy jogging between them.
3.  Run easily for 13 minutes to cool down.

Time check: 12 + (2 x 12) + (1 x 1) + 13 = 50 minutes running; 24 minutes quality.

**Shorter version: 40 minutes total.** Warm up for 10 minutes, run 2 x 9 minutes with a 1-minute jog between them, and cool down for 11 minutes.

Time check: 10 + (2 x 9) + (1 x 1) + 11 = 40 minutes running; 18 minutes quality.

**Larger version: 60 minutes total.** Warm up for 12 minutes, run 3 x 10 minutes with two 2-minute jogs, and cool down for 14 minutes.

Time check: 12 + (3 x 10) + (2 x 2) + 14 = 60 minutes running; 30 minutes quality.

If the first block already feels like a race effort, slow down or end the quality work. The recovery should let you restart smoothly; it does not make an excessive pace appropriate.

## 2.3 Progression

Keep your chosen week while you learn how you respond. Staying with one or two workouts is a valid choice. Before considering more running or more work, look for these signs across repeated weeks:

- You finish the repetitions under control, with enough left for another.
- Your usual easy runs feel easy again, without unusual fatigue carrying into the next workout.
- Soreness is not accumulating, and your sleep and energy in daily life are normal for you.
- The routine fits your available time without repeatedly cutting recovery or missing runs.

One good workout is not enough to judge all of this. If any of these checks are missing, repeat a smaller week or replace a workout with easy running. There is no required date for moving on.

### 2.3.1 Change one thing at a time

| What you want to change | What to do | What stays the same |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Run a little more | Add some easy time to one existing run, then check recovery | Workout count, repetitions, and pace |
| Do a little more controlled work | Replace some easy minutes in your week with controlled work, then repeat and assess | Weekly running time, other workouts, and pace |
| Add another workout | Replace an existing easy run only after the present routine is consistently manageable | Total running time and the other workouts |
| Update pace after a representative race | Try the revised estimate, checking breathing and effort | Repetition count and weekly time |
| Recover from a difficult week | Shorten a workout, make it easy, or rest | No requirement to increase anything |

For example, a runner comfortable with the [four-day week](https://norwegiansingles.run/section2_implementing_the_method.html#transition-week) might extend Wednesday’s easy run from 40 to 45 minutes. The total becomes 185 minutes, with the same 36 quality minutes. Repeat that arrangement and check recovery before changing anything else. Five extra minutes illustrates one small change; it is not a weekly increase rule.

For an example of adding work while keeping weekly running time unchanged, suppose a runner is comfortable with the introductory workout and has four extra minutes available on that workout day. They might change its four three-minute repetitions to five. Keep the 10-minute warm-up, use four one-minute recovery jogs, and finish with 5 minutes easy. The session now takes 34 minutes, including 15 quality minutes. In the three-day example, shortening Saturday’s easy run from 30 to 26 minutes keeps the week at 90 minutes. This is an optional later adjustment, not a required next step. If the workout day has a firm 30-minute limit, keep the original version.

Adding an entire workout is a bigger change. In the five-hour example, changing Saturday from easy to standard C raises quality time from 48 to 72 minutes, even though total running time stays at 300. Do not treat unchanged weekly minutes as unchanged difficulty. Use less work first if needed, or remain on two workouts.

A cool day after weeks of heat can produce faster running at the same effort without proving a new fitness level. Compare similar conditions and repeated sessions before changing a pace estimate. A race used to check fitness [replaces a workout and needs recovery](https://norwegiansingles.run/section5_applicability_distances.html#race-week).

If progress stalls, work through [the recovery and execution checks](https://norwegiansingles.run/section6_benefits_monitoring.html#stagnation) before adding training.

## 2.4 Understanding the time totals

For a basic log, record total running minutes and the minutes spent on work repetitions. Quality minutes are already included in running minutes. You do not need a percentage calculation to run the workouts.

For readers comparing plans, quality share means quality minutes divided by total running minutes. The full five-hour example has 72 quality minutes out of 300 running minutes: 72 / 300 x 100 = 24%. Easy runs, warm-ups, cool-downs, and jogging recoveries all count as running.

Around 20-25% is a planning range discussed in the method, not a minimum to meet or a guarantee that a week suits you. Introductory weeks can sit below it. These percentages also differ from a watch’s time-in-heart-rate-zones report. [Sirpoc discusses the balance in post \#478](https://www.letsrun.com/forum/flat_read.php?thread=12130781&page=23).

### 2.4.1 If you walk or stand during recoveries

The example cards assume jogging recoveries. A set of eight repetitions has seven breaks, followed immediately by the cool-down. If you walk or stand for those breaks, the clock still runs, but this guide does not count those minutes as running.

For example, standard A still takes 50 minutes from start to finish if you stand for all seven one-minute breaks. It then has 43 minutes running and 24 minutes quality. Record the actual running time if comparing weekly totals. For planning your diary, reserve the full 50 minutes either way.

Races and faster workouts also affect recovery, even though their hard minutes are not sub-threshold work. [Log them separately and adjust the week](https://norwegiansingles.run/section5_applicability_distances.html#race-week), rather than trying to preserve the usual quality percentage.
