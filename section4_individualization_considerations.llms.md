# 5  Tools & optional additions

## 5.1 Start with what you have

This chapter is optional. A timer, effort checks, and a simple log are enough to begin; use the sections below if you want to understand a tool you already have or an addition you are considering. Use [Control your effort](https://norwegiansingles.run/section1_core_principles.html#intensity-control) for the instructions during a run. Extra data are useful when they help answer a question about pacing or recovery.

| Tool | What it helps you decide | Main limitation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Pace | Whether you are near the estimate for this repetition length | Terrain, weather, and GPS error affect the reading |
| Effort and breathing | Whether the work still feels controlled | Fatigue, experience, and expectations affect judgment |
| Heart rate | Whether the response is becoming unusually high as work continues | Responds slowly, can rise during steady running, and can be misread by a sensor |
| Blood lactate | How a sampled effort compares with your own tested response | Sampling technique, timing, and personal thresholds matter |
| Running power | Whether output is consistent within a familiar device setup | Device estimates and threshold models differ |
| ZoneSense | How the recorded response develops during steady work | Needs a compatible heart-rate belt and time to respond |
| Training-load scores | How recorded load changes against your history | Cannot determine recovery or predict a race on their own |

### 5.1.1 Pace and effort

If you use a calculator, use the same [Norwegian Singles pace guide](https://lactrace.com/norwegian-singles) across the session cards. A recent race gives it a starting point; effort tells you whether that estimate fits today. When heat or hills make it harder, let the pace slow. Review the run afterward with the conditions noted.

### 5.1.2 Heart rate and lactate

Heart rate is the number of heartbeats per minute. During longer repetitions, look at how it develops at a steady pace. If the response is unusually high and the effort is rising too, slow down. If the reading seems implausible, check the sensor instead of accelerating or braking to follow it.

A lactate meter measures lactate in a small blood sample. It can help a runner who understands when and how to take samples and how to interpret their own response. A fixed reading such as 4 mmol/L (the units used for blood lactate) does not define everyone’s threshold. [Bakken’s account](https://www.mariusbakken.com/the-norwegian-model.html) describes this individuality; his sessions should also be read in the context of his own training.

### 5.1.3 Running power

A running-power device estimates your output in watts. Its readings can be another reference within a consistent setup, especially when pace varies with terrain. Check it against effort and recent sessions before using a new target. Do not transfer watts or percentages blindly between devices.

Some devices use critical power (CP), a calculated boundary between more sustainable and less sustainable efforts, to set training targets. That is a different estimate from LT2 (the second lactate threshold) or maximal lactate steady state (MLSS, the highest tested effort at which blood lactate stays stable). These measures come from different definitions and testing procedures. Treating them as identical can give misleading targets. A [review of CP and MLSS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11927562/) examines why their estimates differ. Follow the testing protocol for the tool you use, and count a hard test as a demanding session that replaces other work.

### 5.1.4 ZoneSense

ZoneSense is a Suunto feature that estimates exercise intensity from the time between heartbeats. Suunto’s [ZoneSense FAQ](https://www.suunto.com/sports/News-Articles-container-page/zonesense-faq/) describes a ten-minute initial period without intensity results and a delayed response to changes in effort. It requires a belt that records the time between individual heartbeats, often called beat-to-beat data. Steady efforts are easier to interpret than short bursts.

A reassuring colour during the opening repetitions is not a reason to increase pace. Use the session’s pace and effort checks, and review the recording afterward. Check the manufacturer’s current compatibility information before buying equipment.

### 5.1.5 Training platforms

A platform can collect the log and show trends. Paid analysis is optional. Start with [what to record](https://norwegiansingles.run/section6_benefits_monitoring.html#monitoring), then read [Training load](https://norwegiansingles.run/section3_training_load.html#training-load) if you want to use scores alongside it.

## 5.2 Core routine and optional additions

The core routine is easy running and controlled quality sessions. You can begin without adding strides, hills, or strength training. If you choose an addition, decide where it fits and what you will reduce if recovery suffers.

| Addition | Purpose and placement | Recovery decision |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Strides | Brief, relaxed faster running after an easy run; allow full easy recovery between efforts | Stop before speed or form deteriorates; omit them when tired |
| Strength | Build strength with familiar movements such as a squat or split squat, hip hinge, and calf raise | Start with a manageable session after running, leaving time to assess soreness before the next quality day |
| Hills or faster intervals | Prepare for a particular event or develop speed during a period of training for that purpose | Replace a quality session and reassess the following days |

For strength work, choose a small number of exercises you can perform comfortably, finish sets before technique fails, and learn unfamiliar movements with appropriate instruction. Begin with one modest session and see whether it affects the next runs before adding more. Record its duration separately in the log; strength work is not part of your running-minute total. Research on strength training and running economy is listed in [the background sources](https://norwegiansingles.run/sources.html#research-background); it does not prescribe one strength routine for everyone.

A known runner’s choice to omit strength or strides is not a universal prohibition. If your results have stopped improving, that does not automatically mean you need harder training. First check [execution and recovery](https://norwegiansingles.run/section6_benefits_monitoring.html#stagnation).

## 5.3 Cross-training

Cycling, swimming, or an elliptical can provide another way to train when running is limited. Record it separately: an hour cycling is not an interchangeable hour of running for the schedules or their quality percentages. If cross-training is part of injury recovery, choose the activity and dose around the injury rather than assuming every alternative is suitable.
