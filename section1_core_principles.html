1  Control your effort

1.1 What the workout terms mean

An interval workout alternates periods of purposeful running with easy recovery breaks. In this guide, a repetition is one period of that purposeful running. For example, “6 x 3 minutes” means six separate three-minute repetitions, with a recovery jog between each pair. It does not mean six runs in a day.

Term What you do
Easy running Run at an effort that lets you hold a comfortable conversation. This includes the longer easy run.
Quality work Run the controlled repetitions described below. “Quality” is just the guide’s name for this part of a workout; easy running matters too.
Recovery jog Jog very easily between repetitions, so you can begin the next one smoothly.
Warm-up and cool-down Run easily before the first repetition and after the last one. These minutes are part of the workout’s total time.

The technical name for the intended work effort is sub-threshold: below the second lactate threshold, often called LT2. That threshold marks an intensity beyond which the effort becomes difficult to sustain. You do not need to measure it to start using the practical checks below; those checks guide effort but cannot confirm your exact threshold.

1.2 How hard should it feel?

During a repetition, breathe more deeply than on an easy run but keep enough breath to say a short phrase. Aim for roughly 5-6 out of 10, where 1 is very light and 10 is your hardest possible effort. Finish feeling that you could complete another repetition at the same effort.

The opening repetition should feel restrained. If you are gasping, straining, or counting down just to survive it, slow down. If the effort keeps rising from one repetition to the next, slow down even if you are still hitting the planned pace. You are practising a workout you can recover from and repeat later in the week.

1.2.1 Choose a starting pace

If you have no recent race result, begin by effort. Start the first repetition only a little faster than your easy jog, let your breathing settle, and use the checks above. It is fine for an early attempt to be too easy. Do not speed up just to reach a particular effort score; record how the whole session felt and use that experience next time.

If you have a recent race result that reflects your current fitness, the LacTrace Norwegian Singles calculator can suggest a pace. Its race-result option is labelled VDOT: enter the race time and distance, then select Analyze. Use the suggestion appropriate to your repetition length. Pace means how long you take to run a kilometre or mile; a larger minutes-per-kilometre number means slower running. Treat the result as a starting estimate and check it against your breathing and effort.

An old personal best or a goal time is not a measure of your current fitness. If the calculator is unavailable or its options are unclear, use the effort instructions above. You can learn the workouts without another tool.

1.2.2 During each workout

  1. Run the easy warm-up before starting the repetitions.
  2. Begin the first repetition at a restrained effort, even when a watch shows a low heart rate.
  3. Jog very easily for the listed recovery time. Repeat the work and recovery until you have completed the planned number of repetitions, or stop the work earlier if needed.
  4. Slow down in heat, wind, or on hills, or whenever the work becomes too demanding. If slowing does not restore control, finish with easy running. Stop for pain or illness symptoms that make continuing inappropriate.
  5. After the last repetition, go straight into the easy cool-down. There is no sprint finish and no extra recovery break to add.

Record how the workout felt and whether your next easy run felt normal. The session cards supply the minutes for each part.

In the original discussion, sirpoc describes using pace estimates and checking the response over the workout, with heart rate rising during the early repetitions. His numbers describe his own training, not everyone’s limits. Original account, post #450.

1.2.3 Why not chase heart rate in the first repetition?

Heart rate takes time to respond. Speeding up to reach a number immediately can make the opening repetitions too hard. Begin at the planned controlled pace and assess the trend as the workout develops. An unusually low reading can also be a sensor problem; it is not a reason to accelerate blindly.

If you have a credible individual threshold-heart-rate estimate, use it as a check on the later repetitions. An age-based maximum-heart-rate formula or a watch’s default zone is not a precise personal threshold. You can run these sessions using pace and effort while you learn your response.

1.2.4 Why are short repetitions faster than long ones?

The pace estimate belongs to a particular work duration and recovery. A three-minute repetition and a twelve-minute repetition need not use the same pace. Another calculator’s continuous threshold pace is answering a different question. Compare the whole session before comparing the numbers.

If you use a pace calculator, use the same guide consistently, and slow down if its estimate feels too demanding. A recovery break does not guarantee that a fast repetition stays below threshold. The session cards keep work duration, recovery, and weekly dose together.

1.2.5 Do you need to measure lactate?

No. There is no universal blood-lactate value that guarantees you are below LT2: Bakken describes substantial individual differences. Testing needs an individual reference and a consistent sampling procedure. See Tools & optional additions if you want to explore this later.

1.3 How to run the easy days

Run slowly enough to talk comfortably in full sentences, with relaxed breathing. Keep the longer run at this same easy effort. “Longer” means longer than your other runs, not faster, and its duration should stay within what you already handle comfortably.

Easy days should leave you ready for the next workout. If you are unusually tired, shorten the run or rest. Keep existing rest days when adapting the example weeks; an easy run is still training.

The practice described by sirpoc uses roughly 70% of maximum heart rate as an upper guide for easy running. That percentage depends on a credible maximum and a reliable measurement. His early explanation, post #170, describes how he used it in his own training. There is no single easy pace that follows from a 5 km result for every runner.

What you notice What to do on this run
Heart rate rises on a hill Slow down or walk the steep part. Choose flatter routes when possible.
Heat makes the usual pace harder Reduce pace and, if needed, duration. Compare effort with similar conditions.
The reading suddenly jumps Check sensor fit and compare with breathing and effort before changing pace.
Easy running feels awkwardly slow Check the maximum-heart-rate estimate and sensor first. Try flatter ground or short walk breaks; shorten the run if the movement remains uncomfortable.
You feel unrecovered from quality work Shorten the run or rest. Reconsider the next quality session using Adjust your week.

Without heart-rate data, use comfortable conversation and relaxed breathing, then check how you feel later that day and before the next workout.

1.4 Consistency & repeatability

Keep the week stable long enough to learn how you respond. You should be recovering between workouts, with easy runs continuing to feel easy. Progression explains how to change the amount of training without changing everything at once.
