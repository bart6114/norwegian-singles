7  Comparing approaches

You can skip this chapter when first trying the workouts. It is for readers comparing plans or deciding whether advice from another method fits their week.

Norwegian Singles offers a repeatable structure with three controlled quality sessions each week. Other approaches vary the mix of workouts or organize them into phases around a race. The useful comparison is what a week asks you to do, how it fits your experience, and how you recover from it.

7.1 Compare the whole plan

Before combining advice from different systems, check:

  • How each plan defines intensity. A numbered zone, threshold pace, or percentage may refer to a different model.
  • Whether total running time includes warm-ups and recoveries, and whether percentages refer to the number of sessions or the minutes spent running.
  • Which session a race, long run, or faster workout replaces.
  • How the plan develops toward the event and allows recovery afterward.

A workout that fits one plan can be too much when added to another. Check what it would replace in your week before trying it.

7.2 Different emphases

Daniels’ Running Formula describes several training intensities and uses them for different purposes. A continuous threshold pace from another system should not automatically become the pace for every singles repetition. Use the session-specific guide when following this routine.

Lydiard-style training uses phases, often called periodization: first building endurance through easy running, then moving toward hill work and race preparation. The singles examples here instead keep the same weekly structure until you deliberately choose an event-specific adaptation. Neither description means that every runner should keep an identical schedule all year.

Pfitzinger’s plans emphasize endurance from mileage and long runs alongside lactate-threshold training. A marathon plan includes the preparation around those sessions. Compare that full build-up with the marathon adaptation, not just a single interval workout.

Power-based plans such as Palladino’s use their own testing and intensity prescriptions. A hard fitness test or faster workout would replace work in a singles week. Power targets and lactate thresholds use different definitions; see the power explanation.

7.3 80/20 and polarized training

An “80/20” split usually describes a mix of mostly easy training and a smaller amount of more demanding work. Polarized training broadly describes an emphasis on easy and hard efforts, with less work between them. Neither label tells you the full schedule or how its percentages were counted. 80/20 Endurance describes its non-easy share as moderate to high intensity. That is not identical to saying every such session must be very hard. Research uses definitions of polarized training that also need to be read in context.

In this guide, the quality share counts only sub-threshold work repetitions. Check the intensity definitions and counting method before comparing that percentage with another plan. A similar percentage can still produce a different week.

7.4 Double threshold

Double threshold places two threshold sessions on the same day within a broader program. Bakken’s original account and 2026 discussion explain his approach and its development. They are accounts of coaching and practice, with their own volume and intensity context.

Adding a second workout to a singles day changes the routine substantially. It is not a required next step, and the success of an elite double-threshold program does not establish that a recreational runner should copy it.

7.5 Choosing a routine

Choose a structure you can execute, recover from, and fit around the rest of your life. Keep it stable enough to assess your response. The available accounts do not establish that this exact singles schedule guarantees better results or fewer injuries than other well-managed training.
